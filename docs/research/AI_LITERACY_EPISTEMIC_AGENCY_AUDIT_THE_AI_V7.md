# AI Literacy, Epistemic Agency and Audit-the-AI Deep Dive V7

Status: V7 mechanistic deep dive
Freshness: 1 October 2026

## 1. Purpose

Pri operates in a world where learners can obtain fluent AI-generated mathematics outside Pri.

A strong mathematics product therefore cannot define success as:

> the learner knows how to get an AI answer.

It must increasingly develop:

- the ability to judge whether an AI claim is credible;
- the ability to verify mathematical reasoning;
- the confidence to disagree with a fluent system;
- the ability to distinguish verified mathematical truth from generated explanation;
- appropriate reliance rather than either blind trust or blanket distrust;
- knowledge of when AI use changes the educational task.

This is **epistemic agency**.

Audit-the-AI should be treated as a mathematical and AI-literacy capability, not a novelty quiz.

## 2. Current primary/secondary AI-literacy authority

The OECD and European Commission released an AI Literacy Framework for Primary and Secondary Education on 18 June 2026.

The framework defines AI literacy as a set of:

- knowledge;
- skills;
- attitudes

that enable learners to:

- understand how AI works;
- critically evaluate AI outputs;
- use AI ethically and creatively;
- make informed decisions about opportunities and risks.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.1787/65cd27d4-en

### Pri consequence

Critical evaluation of AI output is not an optional “future skill.”

It can be treated as part of a modern learner's educational competence.

But Pri should implement it in a mathematically grounded way.

## 3. AI literacy is broader than prompt engineering

Avoid reducing AI literacy to:

- “write better prompts”;
- model-brand trivia;
- memorizing AI definitions.

For Pri, high-value dimensions are:

### System understanding
AI output is generated and fallible.

### Mathematical verification
Can the learner test the claim?

### Evidence/source evaluation
What supports this answer?

### Reliance calibration
When should the learner trust, verify or reject?

### Ethical/data awareness
What information should/should not be shared?

### Agency
Can the learner override the AI and proceed independently?

## 4. Secondary students can be assessed on multiple AI-literacy dimensions

A 2025 study developed and validated an AI-literacy scale with:

- 1,392 secondary students;
- Rasch analysis;
- exploratory factor analysis;
- confirmatory factor analysis.

The proposed KAT framework included:

- AI Knowledge;
- AI Affectivity;
- AI Thinking.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2024.105230

### Pri implication

AI literacy is multidimensional.

But self-report/scale measurement should not replace performance tasks.

Pri's most defensible evidence is:

> can the learner actually detect, explain and correct an AI mathematical error?

## 5. Critical questioning needs pedagogy, not merely access to GenAI

A 2026 Grade 10 classroom action-research study examined how secondary students developed critical questioning with generative AI.

Findings emphasized the importance of:

- instructional design;
- teacher role;
- learner knowledge/disposition;
- the way the GenAI interaction was delivered.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tsc.2025.102043

### Pri consequence

Simply giving a learner a chat box does not teach critical thinking.

Pri should explicitly scaffold:
- questioning;
- verification;
- evidence checking;
- disagreement.

## 6. Mathematical error analysis is a promising direct mechanism

A 2026 undergraduate quantitative-reasoning study used repeated analysis of intentionally erroneous GenAI-generated mathematical solutions.

The study reported large pre/post improvements in:
- mathematics computation;
- GenAI error-detection.

Students also described:
- independent verification;
- recurring error recognition;
- transfer of checking habits.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1892310

Important limitations:
- only about 20 consenting participants;
- 18 paired pre/post observations;
- no comparison group;
- undergraduate setting;
- instructor-curated intentional errors.

The study therefore does **not** establish a causal effect size.

### Pri consequence

It is strong mechanism inspiration for Audit-the-AI, not product efficacy proof.

## 7. Curated AI errors are preferable to live hallucination injection

Pri should never intentionally ask a production model to hallucinate unpredictably in front of a learner.

Instead create:

- expert-authored flawed solutions;
- model-generated candidate errors;
- deterministic verification;
- human/adjudicated validation;
- stable error IDs.

Then use them as controlled learning objects.

This protects:
- mathematical safety;
- reproducibility;
- difficulty control.

## 8. Audit-the-AI canonical task

A strong task can follow:

1. learner sees a question;
2. learner sees a proposed AI solution;
3. learner predicts:
   - valid;
   - invalid;
   - uncertain;
4. learner marks first disputed step;
5. learner explains why;
6. learner repairs or verifies;
7. Pri's math authority checks;
8. learner sees calibrated feedback;
9. later own-work transfer checks whether verification skill generalizes.

The learner should commit before Pri reveals the verified judgment.

## 9. Valid AI answers matter too

If every Audit-the-AI example is wrong, learners learn:

> AI is always wrong.

That is also poor calibration.

Use a mixture:
- fully valid solution;
- valid but unconventional route;
- correct answer with invalid reasoning;
- wrong answer with plausible reasoning;
- subtle domain error;
- incomplete reasoning;
- ambiguous/unresolved case.

Target:
appropriate reliance.

## 10. Separate correctness from optimality

A model can produce:
- valid but inefficient;
- valid but unfamiliar;
- incomplete but promising;
- invalid.

Learners should not learn:

> different from textbook = wrong.

This aligns Audit-the-AI with alternative-route authority.

## 11. Verification tools should come after learner judgment

If Pri instantly displays:

- CAS verdict;
- theorem-prover result;
- answer key;

the learner can outsource evaluation.

Preferred:

learner judgment → learner reason → verified tool check → comparison.

This trains monitoring.

## 12. Appropriate trust is calibrated trust

Two failure modes:

### Over-reliance
Learner accepts incorrect AI output.

### Under-reliance
Learner rejects correct AI output simply because it is AI-generated.

Pri should optimize neither trust nor distrust.

It should optimize **calibrated reliance**.

## 13. Confidence belongs in the task

Ask:

> How confident are you?

Then compare:
- AI correctness;
- learner correctness;
- learner confidence.

Useful outcomes:
- false acceptance;
- false rejection;
- calibrated acceptance;
- calibrated rejection.

This links AI literacy and metacognition.

## 14. Mathematical proof is a special AI-literacy surface

For proof/reasoning:
the final conclusion can be correct while the reasoning is invalid.

Audit-the-AI should include:
- theorem misuse;
- circular reasoning;
- missing condition;
- unjustified equivalence;
- case omission.

This reinforces the distinction between:
- answer;
- reasoning.

## 15. Numerical answers are also useful

For earlier learners:
- fractions;
- signs;
- percentage;
- order of operations;
- equation solving

provide accessible AI-error analysis.

Difficulty can rise with learner evidence.

## 16. Source/evidence literacy

When AI makes a non-mathematical factual claim in a word problem/context, Pri can teach:

- check authoritative source;
- distinguish generated statement from curriculum source;
- ask whether citation actually supports claim.

But Audit-the-AI should remain primarily a mathematics-learning surface unless broader AI literacy is an explicit curriculum objective.

## 17. Pri UI should expose epistemic status

Candidate labels:

### VERIFIED MATHEMATICS
Result checked by admitted mathematical authority.

### AI EXPLANATION
Generated language based on verified facts; may still be imperfect in expression.

### TEACHER / OFFICIAL SOURCE
Human/authority content with provenance.

### UNRESOLVED
Pri does not have sufficient evidence.

Do not imply every AI-generated sentence is equally authoritative.

## 18. The label itself must not become a crutch

If the learner is always told:

> “This is verified”

before reasoning, they may stop checking.

Assessment/Audit mode can withhold the status until after learner commitment.

## 19. Disagreement events are valuable evidence

If learner rejects Pri/AI:

Record:
- claim;
- learner judgment;
- confidence;
- reason;
- final verified state.

This can reveal:
- learner discovered a system error;
- learner holds a misconception;
- alternative valid route;
- ambiguous item.

A disagreement is not automatically learner failure.

## 20. Learner challenge pathway

Every high-impact mathematical response should be challengeable.

Possible interaction:
> “I think Pri is wrong.”

Then:
- preserve original;
- run verifier/alternate route;
- ask for learner reasoning if useful;
- escalate unresolved cases.

This is both safety and epistemic agency.

## 21. Avoid anthropomorphic authority

Bad:
> “Trust me, I checked.”

Better:
> “The algebra verifier confirms these expressions are equivalent under x ≠ 0.”

Ground authority in method, not personality.

## 22. AI literacy and cognitive sovereignty

The PNAS high-school tutoring evidence showed that AI assistance can improve assisted performance while reducing later independent performance under an unrestricted design.

Source:
https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122

AI literacy therefore includes:

> knowing when not to ask AI to do the thinking.

Audit-the-AI can support cognitive sovereignty by making the learner the evaluator.

## 23. AI literacy in assessment

Assessment mode should state:
- whether AI is allowed;
- what kind;
- whether use is part of the construct.

Possible modes:

### No-AI independent assessment
AI inaccessible.

### Tool-permitted assessment
Specific tools allowed.

### AI-evaluation assessment
Using/evaluating AI is the target.

Do not blur these modes.

## 24. AI-generated feedback literacy

Learners need to know how to respond to feedback:

- identify actionable point;
- verify;
- revise;
- reject if unsupported.

A good learner should not automatically accept Pri feedback.

This is a product-strength outcome, not a threat.

## 25. Teacher role

Teacher can use Audit-the-AI activities to:
- discuss verification;
- compare approaches;
- surface over-reliance;
- teach evidence standards.

Teacher dashboard can show:
- class false-acceptance rate;
- common AI error types;
- calibration.

Avoid:
- permanent “AI gullibility score.”

## 26. Guardian role

Guardians can be told:
- AI is fallible;
- Pri teaches checking.

Do not encourage guardians to police every AI interaction.

## 27. AI-literacy benchmark

Construct a frozen benchmark with:
- verified correct AI-like solutions;
- curated incorrect solutions;
- subtle errors;
- unconventional valid routes;
- uncertainty cases.

Measure:

### Detection
Correctly classify valid/invalid.

### Localization
Identify first break.

### Explanation
Give valid reason.

### Repair
Correct it.

### Reliance calibration
Trust correct and reject wrong appropriately.

### Transfer
Apply checking to own work/new AI output later.

## 28. Do not evaluate AI literacy only by self-report

Self-report:
> “I always verify AI.”

can diverge from behavior.

Performance tasks are more defensible.

Use self-report only as complementary evidence.

## 29. Adversarial design

Include errors that are:
- fluent;
- confidently phrased;
- almost correct;
- visually polished.

This better tests whether learners are evaluating the mathematics rather than presentation.

## 30. Avoid unfair trick design

The goal is not:
- fool the learner.

The goal is:
- test verification.

Error should map to a meaningful mathematical principle.

## 31. Difficulty ladder

### AIL-0
Obvious arithmetic error.

### AIL-1
Procedural error.

### AIL-2
Correct result / invalid step.

### AIL-3
Subtle domain condition.

### AIL-4
Valid unconventional method.

### AIL-5
Incomplete evidence / unresolved claim.

Difficulty should align to learner mathematics.

## 32. Experiment programme

### AI-LIT-1
Standard worked example vs curated AI-error audit.

Primary:
delayed mathematical error detection.

### AI-LIT-2
AI-error audit vs ordinary erroneous example.

Question:
does AI framing add value beyond error analysis itself?

This is crucial.

### AI-LIT-3
Structured critical-question prompts vs unguided AI inspection.

Primary:
false-acceptance rate + mathematical transfer.

### AI-LIT-4
Confidence calibration feedback.

Primary:
future appropriate reliance.

### AI-LIT-5
Audit-the-AI → own-work transfer.

Primary:
does checking AI improve learner's detection of their own first break?

## 33. AI framing may be unnecessary

A critical scientific question:

> Is “AI literacy” benefit actually caused by AI framing, or by ordinary mathematical error analysis?

The 2026 undergraduate study cannot answer this because it lacked a control condition.

Pri should compare:
- erroneous worked example labeled “student solution”;
- equivalent flawed solution labeled “AI solution.”

If learning is identical:
use whichever framing has better educational relevance/burden.

## 34. Model-brand independence

Audit-the-AI should teach principles that survive:
- ChatGPT;
- Gemini;
- future systems.

Avoid product-specific training.

Core:
- verify;
- inspect assumptions;
- inspect evidence;
- calibrate reliance.

## 35. Privacy literacy

Learners should also know:
- not to paste private information into arbitrary AI tools;
- AI systems may retain/process data under different terms.

Pri can teach this without collecting more personal information.

## 36. AI literacy is not anti-AI

The target is not skepticism for its own sake.

A learner should be able to say:

> This solution is correct, and here is why.

or:

> This step is invalid, and here is the counterexample.

That is epistemic agency.

## 37. Core decision

Audit-the-AI should become a verified mathematics-learning mechanism whose north star is:

> **calibrated independent judgment — the learner can use AI when useful, reject it when wrong, verify important mathematics, and remain capable when AI is absent.**
